LL What a bus stop is, and what “how many lines call here” means there
Every figure measured from the committed bus payload, its stop locations and the June 2026 feed.
The four marks on this page were drawn on rail, where a station is a building with a name and a platform. A bus stop in this app is none of those things: it is a normalised name that several kerbside poles were averaged into, and the same crossroads often appears twice under two names. Every design here has to survive that, and the multi-line case is both commoner and larger on bus than on rail.
One crossroads, four poles, two keys The pipeline mints a Stop Place from a normalised name and averages the coordinates of every GTFS stop that carries it. At a two-way crossroads that usually merges the two poles on one street into one key, and leaves the other street’s poles under a second key whose name is the same two streets in the other order.
776 pairs of bus Stop Places are the same two streets in the opposite order. They are separate rows in the ranked table, separate marks on the map, and, to a rider, one place with a road between the halves.
The multi-line case is commoner and larger On rail, 14 of 110 stops are served by more than one line and the worst case is four. On bus, 1,497 of 6,785 are, and the worst case is ten. A mark built for four lines has to say what it does at ten.
Grand at 3rd and 6th at Grand each carry ten lines. A ring cut into ten sectors gives each line thirty-six degrees; a fan of ten circles spans a city block; ten bars are a chart, not a stop. Only the badge row survives, and only because it wraps.

The identity question comes before the mark question Every design on this page assumes a station is a place a reader can point at. On bus that assumption is a choice the pipeline already made and defends: a stop’s identity is its normalised name, because Metro’s row order renumbers whenever a line is extended. The consequences are visible above — two keys for one crossroads, a coordinate that is the average of poles up to nine hundred metres apart, and a stop named “Do Not Announce This Stop!” that carries seven lines. None of that is a reason to reopen the ADR, and all of it is a reason to draw bus stops with a mark that never implies a platform.
